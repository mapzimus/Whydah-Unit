The Reference

Why piracy

The core of the driving question. The push/pull framing comes from historian Marcus Rediker (Villains of All Nations, 2004) and other research on what happened to sailors after the Royal Navy let most of them go in 1714. The critique at the bottom matters: Rediker's argument is strong, but not every historian buys it. Treat it as one reading of the evidence, not the only one. This page also carries the ~10-minute pre-paddle brief for the FT2 kayaking day (Thu Jul 16): the four 1717 ship types, the frozen wages, the mortality numbers: why life at sea was brutal, and why people risked piracy.

Privateer vs. pirate: the line was a piece of paper

Same work: chase a ship, board it, take the cargo, fight if you have to. The only real difference was a government license called a letter of marque.

Privateer: legal
Carries a letter of marque from a king or government. Attacks enemy ships, in wartime only. Takes captured ships (“prizes”) to a prize court to be sold legally. A patriot on paper.
Pirate: illegal
No license. Attacks anyone, war or peace. Keeps the loot. A criminal who could be hanged. The Whydah’s crew were here.

When the War of the Spanish Succession ended in 1713, the licenses expired but the sailors didn’t. Many kept doing the only work they knew, now as pirates. Was Bellamy a pirate who never had papers, or a privateer whose papers ran out?

Push Factors: away from alternatives

End of the war (1713–14)
After the War of Spanish Succession ended, the Royal Navy fired most of its sailors and cancelled all the privateer commissions that had let private captains attack French and Spanish ships legally during wartime. Thousands of trained, armed sailors lost their jobs overnight, many stranded in the Caribbean with no way home. Single biggest cause of the Golden Age surge.
From privateer to pirate
A privateer was a private captain with a government license (called a "letter of marque") to attack enemy shipping during a war. Same activity as piracy: board ships, take cargo, fight. The difference was a piece of paper from a king. When the paper expired in 1714 and the work didn't, many privateers kept doing what they'd been trained to do without the license. Bellamy and Hornigold both came up through this path. Useful in S9 and S14: where's the moral line between a privateer and a pirate when the only difference is whose side the king is on this year?
Merchant-ship conditions
Rotten food, payment fraud, cramped quarters, untreated disease, crippling accidents, arbitrary abuse from captains.
Slave-ship crew mortality: 21.6% per voyage (1784–90 House of Commons survey, about 1 in 5 died)
Falling pay
Trade was booming, so there were more sailors than jobs. Merchants used that to pay them less and less.
Royal Navy wages set 1653, not raised for 144 years (until British sailors mutinied in 1797 at a place called Spithead)
Impressment (forced Navy service)
Forced naval service was common practice. The Royal Navy seized men off streets and merchant ships. Deserting the navy to join pirates was a direct path.
Land taken from English farmers (the "enclosure movement")
When wealthy landowners fenced off shared common land in England, small farmers lost their living. Many of those men ended up on ships because there was nowhere else to go.

Pull Factors: what piracy offered

Democratic governance
Pirates elected their captains, voted on matters of moment, and could depose a captain who fell out of favor. Bellamy's crew literally voted Hornigold out in summer 1716. Radical for the era.
Fair share of the loot
A pirate ship's written rules ("Articles") spelled out shares by rank. The captain typically got only about 1.5–2× an ordinary seaman, versus massive captain-to-crew ratios on merchant and naval ships.
Flatter racial hierarchy
Kinkor's research: roughly a quarter of Bellamy's crew, up to 50 men, was Black. Golden Age pirate crews were often 25–33% Black overall. Formerly enslaved men could hold skilled positions unavailable elsewhere.
Compensation for injury
Articles commonly specified set payments for lost limbs or eyes, a form of early workers' compensation, centuries ahead of merchant and naval service. Do the pirate-shares math →
Loosely defended trade
Colonial shipping was rich and poorly protected. Caribbean power vacuum made piracy unusually lucrative and feasible in this window.

Read it yourself: a real pirate crew's Articles

The Whydah's own Articles didn't survive. But we know the crew had a set, because trial testimony confirms it. (A wounded-heart symbol appears among crew engravings found on the wreck, but it isn't proof of the signing.) The closest surviving example comes from Bartholomew “Black Bart” Roberts’ crew, written down in 1724. Here they are, in plain English:

  1. Every man gets a vote on important decisions, and an equal share of the fresh food and drink.
  2. Everyone takes a fair turn at captured goods. Cheating the company → being marooned (left alone on a deserted island).
  3. No gambling for money with cards or dice.
  4. Lights and candles out by eight at night.
  5. Keep your weapons (gun, pistols, and cutlass) clean and ready for service.
  6. No boys or women allowed aboard. (Smuggling a woman on in disguise was punishable by death.)
  7. Deserting the ship, or your post in battle, → death or marooning.
  8. No fighting each other on board; settle quarrels on shore, with sword and pistol.
  9. Nobody quits until each man has earned £1,000. Lose a limb in service and you get 800 dollars from the common fund; smaller injuries paid in proportion.
  10. The captain and quartermaster get 2 shares of a prize; the master, boatswain, and gunner 1½; other officers 1¼; everyone else 1.
  11. The musicians get the Sabbath (Sunday) off; the other six days, only by special favor.

What do you notice? Some of it looks startlingly modern: a vote for every man, injury pay, a captain who earns only twice an ordinary sailor (a Royal Navy captain might earn fifty times as much). And some of it doesn’t: the violence, the marooning, the flat ban on women. Hold both. (Caveat: these come from a 1724 book that may have polished, or even mocked, them, so read them as the best example we have, not a perfect photograph.) Sources: Johnson, A General History of the Pyrates, 1724; Rediker 2004.

The critique. Rediker reads pirates as people pushing back against unfair work and a brutal economy. Other historians push back on him. They argue (1) calling pirates "heroes" overlooks the cruelty some pirates inflicted on the people they captured, and (2) framing the multinational crews as a freedom story can flatten the actual experience of enslaved people on those ships. Both critiques are fair. The push/pull framing explains why people became pirates, and the people who chose piracy also chose theft and violence.

And one more anti-romantic fact. Pirates significantly disrupted the Atlantic slave trade in the 1710s–20s, enough that European powers made wiping out piracy a top priority. But the slave trade rebounded: by the 1730s it had doubled compared to the late 1710s/20s peak of piracy. Pirates interrupted; they didn't stop. We hold all of these halves.
